# DHS-MICS-Consistency-Analysis
**Title:** Assessment of the Consistency of Findings on DHS and MICS Surveys Using Pooled Data: A Study of C-section and Childhood Diseases in 17 LMICs

**Abstract**

**Background**

DHS and MICS are widely used surveys in low- and middle-income countries, but they sometimes give different results. This study checks whether their findings are consistent for cesarean section and childhood diseases. 

**Method**

Data from 17 countries were analyzed using DHS (n = 146,427) and MICS (n = 206,684). Childhood disease was measured as a count (0–4 diseases). A zero-inflated Poisson (ZIP) model was used with survey weights. 

**Results**

In both datasets, most children had no disease (57.55% in DHS vs 53.81% in MICS), while only a small proportion had four diseases (2.12% vs 2.08%). About one-fifth of children had one disease (19.74% vs 19.78%), showing a similar overall pattern. However, important differences were observed. Cesarean section increased disease in DHS (IRR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.03–1.10, p < 0.001), but showed no significant effect in MICS (IRR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.93–1.01, p = 0.15). Other factors showed mixed results between the two surveys. Maternal age and female sex reduced disease in DHS but showed no effect in MICS, while birth order had opposite effects (increased risk in DHS but decreased risk in MICS). In contrast, some factors were consistent across both datasets. Higher maternal education and wealth were associated with lower disease, and children with normal or larger birth size had a lower risk compared to very small children. Child age showed a clear pattern in both datasets, with higher disease in younger children and lower disease in older children. The zero-inflated Poisson model demonstrated the best fit for both datasets (Based on AIC, BIC, and Log Likelihood), confirming the appropriateness of the model.

**Conclusion**

DHS and MICS results are not always consistent. Some factors are reliable across both surveys, but others differ due to methodological differences. Policymakers should compare both sources before making decisions.

**Keywords**

DHS, MICS, Childhood diseases, Cesarean section, LMICs, Survey comparison, ZIP model
